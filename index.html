The core idea

Most ad platforms default to bidding on revenue — a Target ROAS strategy just chases whichever products convert the cheapest, even if those are your worst-margin items. This tool flips that: every recommendation is built around profit, not sales.

Max Profitable CPC = (Price − Cost) × (1 − Buffer%) × Conversion Rate
Target ROAS = Price ÷ [(Price − Cost) × (1 − Buffer%)]

In plain terms: take what you actually profit on one sale, decide how much of that profit you insist on keeping (the Buffer), and that tells you the most you can afford to pay per click before you're spending into your own margin.

Every column, explained

CPCAd Spend ÷ Clicks. What you're actually paying per click right now.
Conv. RateSales ÷ Clicks. How often a click turns into a sale — e.g. 1-in-20 clicks = 5%.
Conf.How much to trust that conversion rate. Built from click volume using a Wilson score interval (the same math behind polling margins of error) — a 5% conversion rate from 10 clicks is noise; from 500 clicks it's real. Low/Med/High reflects that, not the percentage itself.
Margin %(Price − Cost) ÷ Price. How much of each sale is profit before any ad spend.
Max CPCThe highest you could pay per click and still hit your Buffer %, given this product's current conversion rate. This is the number to actually compare your real CPC against.
Target ROASThe same idea as Max CPC, expressed as a ratio — paste this directly into a Google Ads Target ROAS bid strategy.
Net Profit(Sales × Profit per unit) − Ad Spend. What you actually made or lost for the period shown.

Buffer %

This is the one number you're really steering with. It means: "of the profit on a sale, how much do I insist on keeping instead of spending on ads?"

A 20% buffer means you're willing to spend up to 80% of a sale's profit chasing that sale, keeping the other 20% as actual profit. Lower it to bid more aggressively and win more volume; raise it to protect margin more conservatively. Set a default at the top for every product, or override it per-row if one item needs its own number.

Confidence

A conversion rate calculated from a handful of clicks can look identical to one built on thousands — but they don't deserve the same trust. Confidence uses a statistical lower-bound estimate (Wilson score interval) to flag when a row's numbers are still too thin to act on with certainty. Under 50 clicks shows Low; 50–200 shows Med; 200+ shows High. Treat Low-confidence recommendations as a starting hypothesis, not a firm call.

Advanced panel (click "Advanced" on any row)

True cost — Price minus COGS alone usually overstates real margin. Add shipping cost, returns/refund rate, payment processing fee %, and referral/marketplace fee % to get a true margin, and Max CPC / Target ROAS recalculate off that instead.

Stock — if stock on hand is lower than this period's sales pace, the recommendation overrides to "Hold — low stock" regardless of what the profit math says, since profitable bidding is worthless if you can't fulfill it.

Baseline sales (no ads) — your best estimate of what would've sold with zero ads running (from a holdout test or pre-campaign history). This powers the incremental-profit math shown in the Advanced panel: sales that would've happened anyway shouldn't get credited to the ad spend.

Spend-level tests — if you've actually run this product at two or more different budgets/bids, log each result here. The tool shows profit at each tested point and the marginal profit-per-dollar between them — this is the only way to see where returns start diminishing, since that curve can't be predicted, only observed from real tests.

Ad Spend Impact panel

A single before/after comparison: what you'd make with zero ads, next to what you make once ads turn on. The gain banner (Ads added $X / Ads cost you $X) is literally "after profit minus before profit" — which mathematically already accounts for sales that would've happened anyway, without needing a separate calculation.

Planning a budget — you haven't spent yet; give it a budget, expected CPC, and expected conversion rate, and it projects the outcome (assuming ad-driven sales are additional to your baseline — a simplifying assumption, since you can't know real overlap ahead of time).

Reviewing a campaign that already ran — give it your actual spend and actual total sales for the period, and it tells you the truth about what happened, no assumptions needed.

Snapshots

Save snapshot stores your current product list so it's still there next time you open this file. Load last snapshot restores it. Compare to last snapshot shows a ▲/▼ change on Net Profit per row and in the summary bar, so you can see whether things are improving.

This is saved in your browser only — it does not sync to any server, and does not follow you to a different device or a different copy of this file.

Import / export format

Click "Blank template" to download a starter spreadsheet with the exact column headers this tool recognizes. The importer also accepts a few common alternate header names (e.g. "SKU" instead of "UPC / SKU", "Price" instead of "Sale Price ($)"). Only the visible page renders on screen during import — thousands of rows import fine, since the browser never has to hold more than about 100 live rows at once. Export results always includes every imported product, not just what's currently on screen.

What this can't tell you

This tool works from averages — actual CPC and conversion rate don't stay flat as you raise or lower bids, they shift, usually against you at higher spend. Max CPC tells you whether your current numbers are profitable; it can't predict exactly where returns will start diminishing as you spend more. The only real way to see that curve is to actually test different spend levels and log them in the Advanced panel's spend-level tests.

It also can't see anything about competitors — no tool can access another seller's real CPC or conversion data; that's private account information Google never exposes, to anyone.
